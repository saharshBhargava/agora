# What Actually Wins at Major Collegiate Hackathons (2023-2025), with a Fintech/Visa Lens

Research date: 2026-10-17. Method note: Devpost blocks ordinary fetchers (HTTP 403), so winner lists below come from a direct scrape of each hackathon's Devpost project gallery (winner-ribbon entries, name, tagline, team size) plus per-project pages for prize names and "Built With" tags. Keyword saturation counts ("AI", "agent", commerce terms) are my own regex over project name + tagline across every gallery page; they are indicative, not authoritative. HackMIT does not use Devpost, so coverage there is thinner (see Gaps).

---

## Key Question 1: What won at each major hackathon (evidence base of named projects)

### Takeaway
Across ~2,900 scraped projects from 12 editions of 8 hackathons, top overall prizes in 2024-2025 went disproportionately to projects with a physical/sensory "wow" (EEG, robots, projectors, body-as-controller, glasses) or a vivid single-flow demo (HawkWatch's live camera alert, FaceTimeOS calling your Mac), while "AI agent for X" projects mostly won sponsor-specific prizes (Fetch.ai, Bright Data, AWS) rather than the grand prize.

### Inferences
- Across editions, "1st Overall"/grand prizes clustered on projects where the demo is itself the proof: Duet (EEG → sound), kinemo (body → game), HawkWatch (live alert), FaceTimeOS (call your Mac), Alto (bug fixed live), Sustain-ify (point camera at product). Sponsor prizes absorb most of the "agent for X" field.
- Team of 4 is the modal winning team size at every event scraped (e.g., DubHacks '24: 11 of 17 winners had 4 members, 4 had 3, 2 had 2; DubHacks '25: 12 of 22 had 4, 7 had 3, 3 had 2 — from the gallery scrape). Solo and duo winners exist but mostly in sponsor categories.
- Fintech/commerce winners were almost always sponsor-track winners (Visa, RBC, Shopify, Solana, Capital One, Bright Data), not overall winners; the exception is TAShopping-style commerce projects that framed themselves around a physical experience (in-store routing) rather than a payment rail.

### Gaps
- HackMIT 2024 overall grand-prize project name could not be found (HackMIT runs its own submission platform; archive.hackmit.org/2024 is JavaScript-rendered and search surfaced only sponsor/beginner prizes).
- Devpost galleries show a "Winner" ribbon but the ordering is not a ranking; for several events (PennApps XXV, HackHarvard 2025 first-listed winner) I could not confirm which project took 1st overall.
- Judge comments are rare in public sources; most "why it won" statements come from the winning teams themselves.

## Key Question 2: DubHacks specifically — winners 2022-2024, judging culture, team size, demo format

### Takeaway
DubHacks is an expo-style, rubric-scored hackathon (Functionality 30%, Creativity 20%, Track Applicability 20%, Design 10%, 8 points each) with max-4 teams, two broad main tracks plus a long tail of sponsor tracks; judges circulate to tables on Sunday late morning, and winning accounts describe multiple judges testing the project hands-on, so a working, rehearsed demo matters more than a polished deck.

### Cited Findings
- Judging criteria (identical text in 2024 and 2025 Devpost pages): "FUNCTIONALITY Code Functionality and Quality of Execution (8 Points) (30%)... DESIGN User Interface/Aesthetics (8 Points) (10%)... CREATIVITY Unique/New Solution to a Problem with Little Weaknesses (8 Points) (20%)... TRACK APPLICABILITY Relates or does not relate to chosen track (8 Points) (20%)" — [DubHacks '24 Devpost](https://dubhacks24.devpost.com/); [DubHacks '25 Devpost](https://dubhacks25.devpost.com/)
- Judging logistics 2024: "Judges evaluated hacker projects on Sunday, October 13, from 10:30 AM to 2:30 PM PST, using a provided rubric"; judges had to be in person; organizers aimed to recruit ~40 judges and mentors — [UW ECE Advising Blog](https://advisingblog.ece.uw.edu/2024/09/10/request-for-judges-and-mentors-for-dubhacks-24-10-12-to-10-13-posted-9-10/)
- Submission rules: public GitHub link required; "If you are submitting for a sponsor prize, a two-minute demo video is highly encouraged"; initial Devpost submission by Saturday 10:30 PM — [DubHacks '25 Devpost](https://dubhacks25.devpost.com/)
- Team size: sponsor prize text repeatedly says "$500 per team member, up to 4 members" / "max 4 members" — [DubHacks '24 Devpost](https://dubhacks24.devpost.com/); [DubHacks '25 Devpost](https://dubhacks25.devpost.com/)
- Scale: 2023 had 741 participants and 177 projects — [WSU News](https://news.wsu.edu/news/2023/10/26/wsu-tri-cities-computer-programming-club-takes-first-place-at-collegiate-hackathon); 2024 drew "almost 800 students" per the DubHacks site snippet and 577 Devpost participants — [dh24.dubhacks.co via search](https://dh24.dubhacks.co/); [Devpost](https://dubhacks24.devpost.com/); 2025 had 751 Devpost participants and 247 gallery projects — [Devpost](https://dubhacks25.devpost.com/)
- Main tracks: 2023 — Disruption, Synergy, Vitality (+T-Mobile) — [WSU News](https://news.wsu.edu/news/2023/10/26/wsu-tri-cities-computer-programming-club-takes-first-place-at-collegiate-hackathon); 2024 — "Interstellar Connection" (community/relationships) and "Universal Wellness" (health, cybersecurity) — [Devpost](https://dubhacks24.devpost.com/); 2025 — "INVENT - The Innovator" and "GROW - The Advocate", plus DubHacks NEXT (most viable startup; "$200 and expedited interview for DubHacks Next") and Best Freshman project — [Devpost](https://dubhacks25.devpost.com/)
- 2025 sponsor judges included two from Visa (Harshal Shah, Kalyani Nune) alongside AWS/Amazon, Statsig, Centific, Scale AI, Okanagan Specialty Fruits, Atlassian and T-Mobile — [DubHacks '25 Devpost](https://dubhacks25.devpost.com/)
- Statsig's 2025 sponsor rubric explicitly listed "Polish Presentation – Functional demos that clearly explain results" and "Impact Metrics – Show us how your app improves with data" — [DubHacks '25 Devpost](https://dubhacks25.devpost.com/)
- First-hand winner account (Math Sandbox, INVENT 1st, 2025): judging "was delayed significantly"; "a judge informally checked out the project early on"; several judges evaluated the project including a QA engineer who typed "1+1" and exposed a hashtable bug; Gemini parsing failed when the last judge arrived (likely rate limits) so the team "showed a pre-recorded video of a working build and fielded questions anyway"; "We spent our time very strategically, only bothering with features that would matter in our demo"; the team planned the idea for a week beforehand and rehearsed at sunrise; "I didn't see any other teams with a projector" and the projector drew many visitors — [Byron Sharman blog](https://byronsharman.com/blog/dubhacks-2025)
- 2023 overall-style winner RealmDialer came from WSU Tri-Cities (not UW), competing "against 741 individuals and 177 projects" — [WSU News](https://news.wsu.edu/news/2023/10/26/wsu-tri-cities-computer-programming-club-takes-first-place-at-collegiate-hackathon)
- Patient Safety Technology Challenge page shows "Vigil at DubHacks 2024 with judges" (table-side judging photo) — [patientsafetytech.com](https://www.patientsafetytech.com/past-competitions/dubhacks)

### Inferences
- The format is expo/table judging (judges walk to tables, multiple judges per project, hands-on testing), not stage pitches: the ECE blog's 4-hour judging window with ~40 judges for ~170-250 projects, and the winner's account of judges arriving one at a time, both fit science-fair judging. I found no evidence of a finalist stage round at DubHacks.
- Because "Track Applicability" is 20% of the score and judges are often sponsor employees, picking one track and building visibly for it (e.g., Minty hitting every bullet of Visa's prompt) is worth as much as UI polish (10%).
- The Functionality weight (30%) plus hands-on judges means a live, fault-tolerant demo (with a backup video) is the single most score-sensitive asset.

### Gaps
- No official DubHacks "Hacker Guide" rubric breakdown was retrievable (Notion pages render client-side); exact point descriptors unknown.
- No public 2022 DubHacks winner list was found; DubHacks '22 Devpost slug not located.
- No judge-authored commentary on DubHacks judging was found; the UW Daily did not surface in search results for 2024/2025 winners.

## Key Question 3: Visa-sponsored hackathon challenges 2024-2025 — what won and how Visa APIs were used

### Takeaway
At collegiate events Visa's presence in 2025 was a low-cash sponsor track (iPads at DubHacks, $200 gift cards at Cal Hacks, $250 gift cards per member at HackHarvard), and the winners used little or no real Visa API — DubHacks explicitly allowed Stripe/PayPal sandbox or mock payments — while Visa's own startup program (VEI) rewarded payments-infrastructure plays (failed-subscription recovery) judged on "value added for Visa / partnership opportunity"; Visa's 2025-2026 agentic-commerce challenges judge whether "an agent takes meaningful action" and "payments are handled clearly."

### Cited Findings

## Key Question 4: How saturated are "AI agent that shops/books/plans for you" projects in late-2025 hackathons?

### Takeaway
By late 2025 roughly a third to nearly half of all submissions at large collegiate hackathons mention AI in their one-line tagline, and "agent" mentions rose ~5-10x year over year (Cal Hacks: 10 → 88 projects; Hack the North: 4 → 19; DubHacks: 0 → 9), yet "agent"-tagged projects won a smaller share of non-sponsor prizes; judges and participants publicly describe pitch-driven events as rewarding "vibe coded slop" with polished front-ends, and a growing counter-movement (peer voting, engineering-practice rubrics, "De-Vibed" hackathons) is emerging.

### Inferences
- "Agent" is now the default framing for roughly 10-15% of all Cal Hacks/TreeHacks submissions and an outsized share of sponsor-funded tracks; the only agent projects that reach "1st Overall" are ones whose agent acts in an unexpected surface (FaceTimeOS: your Mac answers a FaceTime call; Alto: ships a mobile bug fix) rather than shopping/booking/planning.

- Judge fatigue is documented in 2026 sources rather than 2025, but the Devpost counts show the saturation itself arrived in 2025; the complaint lags the phenomenon by one cycle.

### Gaps
- No organizer blog post or judge statement from a named 2025 collegiate hackathon explicitly complaining about "agent slop" was found; the strongest judge-voice sources are 2026 (HackEurope, JetBrains, Semgrep).
- Keyword counts undercount AI projects that omit "AI" from the tagline and overcount via words like "copilot"; treat percentages as ±5 points.

## Key Question 6: Concrete demo-structure, polish and code-verification advice from judges and winners

### Takeaway
Judges say the pitch decides most winners: lead with the problem, show something working within ~90 seconds, demo one flow end-to-end with everything pre-filled or mocked, be candid about what does not work, and rehearse out loud; judges do test projects hands-on and probe code the team cannot explain, so design-first polish (Figma) helps only on top of a demo that survives a judge typing "1+1".

### Cited Findings
- Katherine Druckman (JetBrains x Codex Hackathon judge, interviewing fellow judges): "A strong project with a confusing demo loses to a simpler project that the judges understand"; Jono Bacon: "You have to be able to show something working within about 90 seconds"; Avi Press: "Put the judge in the shoes of the user and walk them through what happens... Be very direct about what works, what doesn't"; Colin Lowenberg (Nebius): "Your demo should be the focus. It should show what your app does in one flow. If it's too long, cut down on your features" and "Mock everything you can and make sure all your forms are filled"; Bonnie Xu (OpenAI): start from "what's something I can build now that I probably couldn't have built before?", one clear "oh, this is possible now" moment beats a feature tour; Jan-Niklas Wortmann: "Read the rules before... what the judges are probably looking out for," and "shipping a good demo matters more than having the most reliable, highest-quality code" — [JetBrains blog](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)
- Judges verify by using the product: at DubHacks '25 "a QA engineer entered '1+1,' which exposed a flaw"; API failure during the last judge was covered by a pre-recorded video — [Byron Sharman blog](https://byronsharman.com/blog/dubhacks-2025)
- Judges verify by asking about code: a participant lost because "I trusted the AI generated code without fully reading or understanding it" and "couldn't answer the judges' questions well" — [Substack](https://shrit.substack.com/p/lost-so-bad-got-humble-again); HackerEarth tips say judges will probe any part of the code the team can't explain and recommend writing the ~90-second demo script before coding — [HackerEarth](https://www.hackerearth.com/blog/10-tips-win-hackathon)
- Expo dynamics: "Judges each stopped by for only a few minutes to see a demo and hear the product's story. - Working demo as the differentiator: EyeCraft's Xiaole Su: "One thing that really made us stand out is how well the demo worked and how everything flowed together" — [Khoury](https://www.khoury.northeastern.edu/khoury-undergrads-win-three-categories-at-prestigious-mit-hackathon)

## Key Question 7 (Synthesis): What distinguishes winners from "AI agent for X", what judge psychology looks like in 3 minutes, and what fintech sponsors reward

### Takeaway
Winners pair a first-20-seconds sensory or narrative hook with one reliable end-to-end flow that the judge can touch; "AI agent for X" loses when the demo is a chat log, and wins only when the agent acts in a surprising surface or completes a verifiable transaction. Fintech sponsors in 2024-2025 rewarded trust-and-control narratives (fraud, MFA, parental controls, chargebacks), two-sided demos, and ideas that plausibly grow the sponsor's network, far more than deep API usage.

### Cited Findings (cross-references)

### Inferences (the practical playbook)
1. **Open with the artifact, not the problem slide.** Every grand-prize winner above can be understood from a 5-second glance at the table (EEG cap, projector, robot, a FaceTime call to a laptop, a live camera feed flagging a fainting person). For a fintech/commerce project, the equivalent is a physical or two-device moment: a real card tap, a second phone receiving the payout, a merchant screen lighting up as a shopper checks out.
2. **Make the agent do something a judge can verify in the room.** Agent projects that won showed a completed action with evidence (a booked hotel receipt, a merged PR, a deployed fix). Shopping/booking/planning agents that end in a recommendation read as generic. For Visa-style agentic commerce, the demo should be: agent is given a goal → agent transacts under a visible control (spend limit, parental approval, risk score) → settlement shows up on the other side.
3. **Design the demo for a judge who will type "1+1".** Pre-fill forms, mock slow APIs, cache LLM calls, keep a recorded fallback, and rehearse the handoff between teammates (DubHacks and Cal Hacks winners all describe multi-judge, few-minutes table visits).
4. **Hit the sponsor prompt's bullets literally and visibly.** Visa track winners in 2025 mapped 1:1 to the prompt text (nostalgia + parental controls + incremental payout; SMB chargebacks + plug-and-play MFA). Name the bullets on a card at the table.
5. **Pitch the sponsor's upside, not the API.** Visa's own criteria ask what the idea adds to Visa; a winning line sounds like "creates N new payment moments per family per week with parental controls" or "removes chargeback friction for SMBs who can't afford enterprise tools," not "we called the Visa Direct sandbox."
6. **Avoid the generic-AI tells.** Chat-box-first UI, "AI-powered X" taglines, and features you cannot explain line-by-line all read as slop in 2025-2026 judging; a non-chat interface (dashboard, camera, physical device, game) plus one explainable model call reads as engineering.
7. **Team of 3-4, one presenter-coordinator, Figma before code.** Modal winning team size is 4; Minty and TAShopping both credit an explicit presentation coordinator and design-first planning.

- Judge-side commentary specific to Visa or payments sponsors at collegiate events is absent; the fintech inferences lean on Visa's published rubrics and the content of winning submissions.
